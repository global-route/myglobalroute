A TikTok ad from an account called World Wide Service has 69.4 million views. It shows a smiling young African man and promises: work in Serbia, no experience required, manufacturing, farms, construction, free accommodation and meals, official employment.

At the bottom: "Paid program — for candidates ready to proceed."

69.4 million views means this ad has reached a significant portion of the Nigerian and African social media audience. This post explains what Serbia's actual work permit system looks like — and what the phrase "paid program" means.

## What the ad is selling

The ad is selling placement services — you pay the recruiter or agency a fee, and they claim to arrange a job and travel to Serbia. This is the paid-program model.

The specific red flags in this ad:

**"Paid program"** — the candidate pays upfront. In legitimate international recruitment, the employer pays recruitment costs. Workers do not pay to get a job. This is the ILO standard and is consistent with Serbian labour law.

**No salary stated** — the ad mentions free accommodation and meals but no actual wage. In Serbia, manufacturing and agricultural workers earn approximately RSD 40,000-60,000/month (approximately USD 350-550). This is not a high-income destination.

**No visa type named** — the ad does not mention which Serbian work permit applies, what the employer's name is, or what the job contract looks like.

**No experience required + free accommodation** — this combination is a standard pattern in unskilled migration ads targeting vulnerable applicants. It is designed to lower the barrier to payment, not to accurately describe the job.

## Serbia's actual work permit system

Serbia is not an EU member state. It has its own work permit system for non-EU foreign workers.

**Work permit (radna dozvola):** Required for non-EU nationals working in Serbia. The employer applies on behalf of the worker through the National Employment Service (NES). The worker does not pay for the permit.

**Types of work permits:**
- Employment permit — for workers with a job contract
- Posted worker permit — for workers sent by a foreign employer
- Self-employment permit — for business owners

**Process:**
1. Employer in Serbia offers a job contract
2. Employer applies for work permit at NES
3. Worker applies for a long-stay visa (D visa) at the Serbian embassy
4. Worker travels and registers with local police within 24 hours of arrival

**Salary:** Serbia's minimum wage is approximately RSD 40,000/month (USD 350). Manufacturing and agricultural roles typically pay RSD 40,000-70,000/month. This is significantly lower than Western European destinations.

**Verify at:** `mup.gov.rs` (Ministry of Interior), `nsz.gov.rs` (National Employment Service)

## Is Serbia worth considering?

Serbia is a real country with a real economy. It has a growing manufacturing sector (automotive, electronics, food processing) and agricultural industry. Foreign workers do work there legally.

However, it is not a high-income destination. The salary range is comparable to some African countries and significantly below Western Europe. The cost of living in Serbia is lower than Western Europe, but the income gap with destinations like Germany, Ireland, or the Netherlands is large.

If you are considering Serbia specifically because of a paid-program ad, stop. If you are genuinely interested in Serbia as a work destination, the path is:

1. Find a legitimate Serbian employer directly — `infostud.com` is Serbia's main job board
2. Confirm the employer will apply for your work permit at NES
3. Confirm no upfront fees are required from you
4. Verify the job contract before travelling

## The pattern to recognise

The Serbia ad is one instance of a pattern that appears across many countries — Mongolia, Qatar, Gulf states, and now Serbia. The pattern is:

- High-reach social media ad (millions of views)
- Promises no experience required
- Mentions free accommodation and meals
- Does not state salary clearly
- Requires upfront payment from the candidate
- Uses urgency language ("candidates ready to proceed")

The country changes. The pattern does not.
